01 · ABOUT ME

I started in engineering. I switched to data, and it was the right call.

I was enrolled in engineering, and it never felt right. When the Data Science program was introduced, I switched. My first real data job was almost two years at Karan Surgicals, a surgical-products manufacturer, where I put together weekly expense and materials reports and worked out what each extra unit actually cost to make. That's where I learned that a spreadsheet only matters if someone changes what they do because of it.

Since then I've worked as a data analyst intern in healthcare, a data engineer intern on industrial sensor data, and an AI automation intern building a policy-search assistant. I've also spent three years on the IT help desk at York, and weekends guiding crowds through Netflix's Stranger Things: The Experience. Both taught me to explain technical things calmly to people who need an answer now.

WHAT I LIKE

A dataset nobody has looked at closely yet, especially Canadian public data. My projects usually start with something I noticed in everyday life, like a protest outside my own campus or the price of groceries.

WHAT I DON'T LIKE

Big numbers with no comparison. If something went up 468%, my first question is "compared to what?" In one of my projects, that exact number turned out to be the survey getting bigger.

WHAT I'M LOOKING FOR

An entry-level data analyst, BI or data engineering role in the Greater Toronto Area, starting May 2027.

04 · REFLECTIONS

Good analysis is wasted if the writing around it is weak.

THE OLD WAY

"This project uses Statistics Canada Table 18-10-0245-01 to build a SQL star schema..."

WHAT I WRITE NOW

"Ontario's weekly basket rose 16.4%. Five swaps save $501 a year."

  1. 1Lead with the answer. The finding comes first, then the method.
  2. 2Write for one reader. A recruiter and a hiring manager want different things, so I rewrite for each.
  3. 3Be specific or cut it. "Improved efficiency" says nothing. "3 hours to 45 minutes" can be checked.
  4. 4Say what you got wrong. Showing where I checked myself makes the rest more believable.

What I'm taking with me: I write the one-sentence finding before I start. If I can't write that sentence, I don't understand the question yet.

Speaking up. One of my classes this year gave marks for participation, so I had to talk in front of people every week. It was uncomfortable at first, but it built real confidence, and the presentations made it stick.

Learning for the job, not the grade. The course was built around what I'll face at work: presenting, building a resume, and making an e-portfolio like this one. My instructor reviewed my resume and showed me what a recruiter needs to see and what was just extra. That review shaped the resume I'm sending out now, and this site too.
